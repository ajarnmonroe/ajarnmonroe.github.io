Study Debrief

ELT Leadership and Instructional Practices Survey

Thank you for participating

Thank you for contributing your professional judgment to this doctoral research study.

The instructional behaviors you evaluated were developed from a qualitative analysis of fictional teachers in eight selected musical narratives.

The identities of the teachers, the productions in which they appear, and the surrounding narrative context were not disclosed before the rating task. This was done so that each behavior could be evaluated on its own, without judgments being influenced by a particular character, actor, story, or narrative outcome.

Your survey response has already been submitted. The information on this page is provided as a debrief and does not affect your recorded responses.

How the fictional teachers were analyzed

The qualitative phase of the study examines the portrayals through five empirical categories:

  • Teaching Style
  • Instructional Behaviors
  • Classroom Dynamics
  • Approaches to Authority
  • Depicted or Implied Learning Outcomes

Together, these categories provide a way of examining what the fictional teachers do, how they interact with learners, how authority operates in instructional settings, and what kinds of learner outcomes are depicted or implied within the narratives.

The findings are also considered in relation to four theoretical perspectives:

Theory

Transformational Leadership

Used to consider influence, motivation, intellectual stimulation, and individualized attention within teacher–learner interactions.

Theory

Classroom Management

Used to examine how teachers organize, regulate, and maintain instructional activity and participation.

Theory

Positioning Theory

Used to examine how rights, responsibilities, relationships, and authority are constructed and negotiated through interaction.

Theory

Pedagogical Content Knowledge

Used to examine how teachers represent, explain, demonstrate, and adapt subject matter or skills to make them accessible to learners.

The eight fictional teachers

The King and I (1956)

Anna Leonowens

Anna is employed to educate the children of the King of Siam, including instruction in English and other Western subjects. Her teaching includes explanation, questioning, modeling, comparison, and the negotiation of authority within the royal court.

The narrative depicts considerable learner participation and engagement, although it does not provide a single simple academic endpoint by which her teaching can be judged.

Goodbye, Mr. Chips (1969)

Arthur Chipping

Mr. Chipping is a long-serving Latin and classics teacher at an English boys' school. His teaching includes academic expectations, questioning, correction, discipline, and changing relationships with generations of students.

His story emphasizes the development of teacher–student relationships across a long career rather than a single measurable learner outcome.

The Music Man (1962)

Harold Hill

Hill presents himself as a music and band instructor and promotes his unusual “Think System”, in which learners are encouraged to mentally rehearse music rather than depend primarily on conventional instrumental practice.

The boys eventually perform publicly, and their parents respond enthusiastically. The portrayal leaves considerable room for different judgments about what counts as a meaningful instructional outcome.

My Fair Lady (1964)

Henry Higgins

Higgins is a phonetics and pronunciation specialist who undertakes intensive speech training with Eliza Doolittle. His approach includes modeling, repetition, correction, demanding practice, and sometimes harsh treatment of the learner.

The narrative later shows Eliza successfully demonstrating the speech and social performance that formed the central goal of the training.

Sarafina! (1992)

Mary Masombuka

Mary is a history teacher working with students in apartheid-era South Africa. Her classroom combines historical content, questioning, learner participation, creativity, performance, and reflection on social and moral issues.

The portrayal emphasizes engagement, participation, reflection, and relationships rather than providing one simple academic endpoint.

Matilda the Musical (2022)

Miss Honey

Miss Honey is a primary-school classroom teacher responsible for general academic instruction. She encourages participation, offers reassurance and peer support, and recognizes opportunities for learners to work beyond the expected level.

Matilda demonstrates exceptional academic ability, although that ability is already evident when Miss Honey begins teaching her and cannot simply be treated as an outcome caused by the teacher.

Billy Elliot the Musical Live (2014)

Mrs. Wilkinson

Mrs. Wilkinson is a ballet and dance teacher who recognizes Billy's potential and provides demanding practical coaching through demonstration, repeated practice, correction, individualized guidance, and audition preparation.

The narrative later shows Billy being accepted into the Royal Ballet School following his preparation and audition.

Mean Girls (2024)

Ms. Norbury

Ms. Norbury is a mathematics teacher who combines academic challenge with individual support and guided reflection on student behavior. Cady is shown to be highly capable in mathematics, although she deliberately underperforms for social reasons.

Later, Cady receives a score of 100 and takes part in and wins the Mathletes state championship.

Interpreting outcomes

The study treats learning outcomes as depicted or implied. It does not assume that a particular teacher behavior directly caused a particular narrative result.

The portrayals also illustrate a familiar issue in educational leadership: an outcome may look different depending on who is evaluating it and what is being measured.

Harold Hill's students and their parents, for example, may place value on participation, confidence, enjoyment, and community pride, while a professional music educator may place greater emphasis on technical proficiency, practice, and musical accuracy.

Cady's experience illustrates a different problem. Her failed quizzes appear to indicate weak academic performance, although she already understands the mathematics and is deliberately providing incorrect answers for social reasons.

In that sense, a KPI can provide useful information without necessarily telling the whole story about teaching or learning.

How your responses contribute

Your responses provide the next stage of the study by showing how practicing ELT leaders and administrators in Saudi Arabia evaluate decontextualized instructional behaviors derived from these fictional portrayals.

These judgments will be considered alongside the qualitative findings and the theoretical perspectives described above to examine possible implications for instructional leadership, classroom authority, teacher support, and pedagogical practice within Saudi ELT.

If any of these productions are unfamiliar to you, you are warmly encouraged to watch them and consider how the behaviors you evaluated appear within their original narrative contexts.

Thank you again for taking part in this research.
